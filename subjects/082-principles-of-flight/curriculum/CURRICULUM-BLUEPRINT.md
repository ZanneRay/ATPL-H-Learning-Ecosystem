# 082 Principles of Flight (Helicopters) — Curriculum Blueprint

## Role of this subject

Principles of Flight is the first complete implementation of the ATPL(H) Learning Ecosystem. The subject will be used to validate the educational model, workflow, HeliLab integration and assessment approach before expansion to other subjects.

## Design target

The existing 32-hour theoretical course is redesigned as a coherent learning journey rather than a sequence of content presentations followed by a single examination.

The curriculum must preserve EASA theoretical-knowledge coverage while deliberately developing the student's ability to:

- predict aerodynamic consequences;
- explain helicopter behaviour using causal aerodynamic reasoning;
- interpret changing flight conditions;
- connect theory to the H145 D3 where useful;
- recognise aerodynamic hazards and limits;
- make and justify operationally relevant decisions;
- identify and correct weaknesses in their own mental model.

## Provisional module architecture

The final lesson count and allocation will be validated against EASA LO coverage and the available 32 classroom hours.

| Module | Core question | Representative content |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Build the aerodynamic model | Why can a rotor produce useful aerodynamic force? | Air, relative airflow, pressure, lift, drag, AoA, aerofoils |
| 2. Make the rotor work | How does a rotating blade become a controllable rotor? | Blade element theory, induced velocity, blade motion, pitch, flapping |
| 3. Hover, climb & descend | What changes when the rotor moves air vertically? | Hover, power, ground effect, climb, descent, VRS |
| 4. Transition to forward flight | Why does the helicopter change as airspeed builds? | Translational lift, transition effects, dissymmetry, flap-back |
| 5. Fast & manoeuvring flight | Where do the aerodynamic limits come from? | Power, RBS, compressibility, turning flight, load factor |
| 6. Stability & control | Why does the helicopter respond the way it does? | Static/dynamic stability, CG, SAS/AFCS, rotor response |
| 7. Autorotation & energy management | How can the rotor sustain itself without engine power? | Autorotation regions, energy exchange, forward autorotation, flare |

Tail-rotor aerodynamics and associated hazards should be integrated where they best support the conceptual learning sequence, with a dedicated learning sequence if LO mapping and instructional coherence require it.

## Standard learning sequence

Every major concept should be designed using a common pattern, adjusted where necessary:

1. **ORIENT** — operational hook or problem.
2. **PREDICT** — student commits to an expected outcome.
3. **BUILD** — concise theory and causal model.
4. **EXPLORE** — manipulate the concept in HeliLab or another suitable activity.
5. **EXPLAIN** — student articulates why the observed result occurred.
6. **APPLY** — use the concept in a helicopter scenario.
7. **CHECK & REFLECT** — formative check, feedback and self-correction.

## Example — opening learning sequence

### Operational problem

> You need to establish and maintain a stable hover. What must the rotor actually do to support the helicopter, and what changes the amount of thrust and power required?

### Theory foundation

Students need sufficient foundational aerodynamics before meaningful scenario discussion. The redesign therefore does **not** replace theory with discussion. Instead, theory is taught in smaller purposeful blocks that immediately feed prediction, exploration and application.

### HeliLab role

Students should be able to change selected variables and see the effect on relative airflow, angle of attack, aerodynamic force, rotor thrust and/or power. The activity should expose misconceptions rather than simply animate the textbook.

### Evidence of learning

A student should ultimately be able to explain a causal chain, for example:

`condition changes → local airflow changes → AoA / aerodynamic force changes → rotor effect changes → aircraft consequence → appropriate pilot consideration`

This causal reasoning pattern should recur throughout Subject 082.

## Assessment philosophy

The course should not rely solely on one end-of-course multiple-choice event for evidence of learning. The architecture should distinguish:

- **diagnostic checks** — identify prior knowledge and misconceptions;
- **formative checks** — frequent low-stakes feedback during learning;
- **scenario/application evidence** — explanations, predictions and decisions;
- **summative assessment** — formal controlled assessment through the approved assessment environment.

The exact LPlus implementation and regulatory constraints remain to be specified and verified before the assessment design is frozen.
